Attention Internals: KV Cache & GQA

Your question is the right one and it points at a real confusion: does V depend on K and Q? No — Q, K and V are three independent projections of the same input. They only meet inside the attention op. Nail that and the cache, and GQA, both fall out cleanly.

01 The crux

Q, K, V are siblings, not a chain

It’s tempting to read “attention” as Q → K → V flowing in sequence. It isn’t. From a token’s residual vector x, the model applies three separate linear maps in parallel:

three independent projectionsQ = x·WQ　　K = x·WK　　V = x·WV
// different weight matrices, computed at the same time, no dependency between them

What does depend on Q and K is the set of attention weights — and those then act on V:

the attention operationattention = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d ) · V
// Q·K decides HOW MUCH to attend; V is WHAT gets summed up

So the right mental model: Q and K are the “addressing” system — a query compares against keys to decide where to look. V is the “content” that gets retrieved. V isn’t built from Q or K; it’s an independent payload that the Q·K weights pick out. Think of it like a soft dictionary lookup: keys are what you match against, values are what you read out.

02 The cache

Why we cache K and V — but never Q

During generation you produce one token at a time. To compute attention for the new token, you need its own single Q, and you need K and V for every previous token so the new query can look back over the whole context.

Here’s the asymmetry. A past token’s K and V get re-used at every future step — every new query attends over all of them. But a past token’s Q is used once, at the moment that token was generated, and never again. So caching K and V saves enormous recomputation; caching Q would be pointless.

Two phases worth naming Prefill processes the whole prompt at once, filling the cache — compute-heavy and parallel. Decode then emits one token per step, each reading the full cache — sequential and memory-bandwidth-bound. The cache grows by one K and one V per token per layer, so it swells linearly with length and, for long contexts, becomes the dominant memory cost — often larger than the model weights.

03 Your GQA question

GQA shares K and V — across groups of queries

To your exact words — “are you just sharing V?” — no: a “KV head” is a (key projection + value projection) pair, and GQA shares that whole pair. What stays separate is the queries. Three configurations, same skeleton:

Why it barely costs quality Intuition: the queries do most of the discriminative work — deciding what to look for — while keys and values are more redundant across heads. So sharing a KV pair across a handful of queries loses little, whereas MQA (one KV for all queries) is aggressive enough to dent quality. GQA is the sweet spot nearly every modern model now picks.

04 Back to your build

Why an RL person cares about all this

RL rollouts are generation, so the decode phase you just dissected is where your training wall-clock goes — especially for long chain-of-thought, where the cache grows with every reasoning token. A 10k-token reasoning trace means a 10k-long KV cache per sequence, times your group size G, times your batch. That’s why GQA, paged caches, and inference engines matter for the rollout stage, and why long-CoT RLVR is expensive in a way short-answer RLVR isn’t.

The one-line bridge Faster, smaller KV cache → cheaper rollouts → more samples per GPU-hour → more RL. Inference efficiency is RL throughput. The architecture deltas from the first primer aren’t trivia; they set the cost of every completion the verifier ever scores.

05 Quiz · three

Check yourself

Q1 SOLVE Restate, in your own words, why “does V depend on K and Q?” is the wrong frame — and what actually depends on Q and K. ▸

Q, K and V are three independent linear projections of the same input vector x — different weight matrices, computed in parallel, no dependency among them. V is a self-contained “payload.” What depends on Q and K is the attention weights: softmax(Q·Kᵀ/√d) decides how much each position attends to each other. Those weights are then applied to V in a weighted sum. So V meets Q and K only at the final step, as the thing being summed — it is never computed from them. Q/K = addressing; V = content retrieved.

Q2 SOLVE Precisely why is it the “KV cache” and not the “QKV cache”? What property of Q makes it cache-worthless? ▸

A past token’s K and V are read by every future query — each new token attends back over all prior K’s and V’s — so storing them avoids recomputing the whole prefix each step. A past token’s Q, by contrast, is used exactly once (when that token was generated) and never again: future steps only need the current token’s query. Caching something you’ll never re-read buys nothing. Hence K and V are cached; Q isn’t. The cache grows by one K and one V per token per layer, which is why long contexts get memory-bound.

Q3 SOLVE A model has 32 query heads. Compare the KV-cache size under MHA, MQA, and GQA-8, and explain why query count doesn’t enter the cache size. ▸

Cache size scales with the number of KV heads, because only K and V are stored. So: MHA = 32 KV heads (one per query) → baseline; MQA = 1 KV head shared by all 32 queries → 32× smaller; GQA-8 = 8 KV heads, each shared by 4 queries → 4× smaller than MHA (and 8× larger than MQA).

Query count doesn’t appear because queries are never cached — only the current step’s query is computed and immediately consumed. You can keep all 32 query heads for full expressiveness at zero cache cost; the only lever on cache size is how many distinct K/V pairs you keep. GQA exploits exactly that: shrink KV heads, keep query heads.

Takeaway

Q, K, V are siblings projected from the same input — Q·K makes the weights, V is the content they retrieve. We cache K and V because every future query rereads them; we skip Q because it’s used once. GQA shrinks the cache by sharing whole K/V pairs across groups of queries, while the queries themselves stay free and independent.
