Beyond the GPU

A GPU is a general parallel processor that happens to be great at ML — graphics heritage and all. Once you accept the memory wall from the last lesson, you can design silicon that attacks it head-on. Each alternative — TPU, LPU, wafer-scale — is a different answer to “where is the bottleneck, and how do we delete it?”

01 The premise

Specialize to beat the memory wall

NVIDIA still holds roughly 80% of the market, and the reason challengers exist anyway is the last lesson: if the bottleneck is data movement, a chip that fixes your specific movement problem can crush a general GPU on that workload. The catch is always the same — you give up flexibility and the CUDA ecosystem. So alternatives win in niches, and most fiercely in inference, where the CUDA moat is thinnest and latency or cost-per-token is everything.

02 The architectures

Three real departures

TPU — the systolic array (Google)

Google’s tensor processing unit hard-wires matrix multiply into a systolic array: a grid of multiply-accumulate cells through which data rhythmically flows, so each value loaded gets reused across the whole row/column before leaving — maximizing arithmetic intensity by design. It’s the most mature non-GPU silicon (Google’s been at it since 2015), the latest generation (Ironwood) is described as roughly Blackwell-class, and it runs in 10,000+ chip clusters. The cost: it’s Google-Cloud-only, and it speaks XLA, not CUDA.

LPU — determinism over flexibility (Groq)

Groq’s Language Processing Unit makes a radical choice: no HBM at all, just a large pool of on-chip SRAM, and a fully deterministic, compiler-scheduled pipeline with no dynamic caches or schedulers. Because every operation’s timing is known at compile time, latency is ultra-predictable and very low — the headline is hundreds-to-800+ tokens/sec on a single stream. The price is brutal capacity limits (SRAM is small), so a model is spread across many chips, and it’s inference-only.

Cerebras — wafer-scale (delete the network)

Cerebras builds the chip at wafer scale — one dinner-plate of silicon with ~4 trillion transistors and ~900,000 cores, instead of dicing the wafer into hundreds of GPUs. The point is to eliminate inter-chip communication, the bottleneck from the last lesson: if the whole model fits on one wafer, there’s no slow network to cross, and on-wafer SRAM bandwidth is enormous. The constraint is equally radical — power, cooling, and the requirement that the workload fit the wafer’s shape.

03 The shape of the market

Train on GPUs, infer on anything

The pattern across all of it: NVIDIA dominates training (complex, custom-kernel-heavy, where CUDA is deepest), while the inference market is fragmenting fast — that’s where alternatives offer best-in-class cost or latency on specific transformer workloads. Even the hyperscalers play both sides: they buy NVIDIA and build their own ASICs (Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA) to cut cost and reduce dependence. A common strategy is literally “train on NVIDIA, serve on whatever’s cheapest per token.”

Tie-back to your rack-scale work This is the decision underneath your hardware evaluations. The MI300X/MI325X vs B200 question, the serving-stack choice — they’re instances of match the chip to where your workload sits on the roofline. Memory-bound decode at scale rewards huge bandwidth and cheap tokens; training rewards FLOPs, interconnect, and the CUDA stack. There’s no “best chip,” only best-for-this-workload-at-this-scale-under-this-power-budget.

Why this matters beyond procurement Hardware shapes models, not just budgets. FP8/FP4 support pushed low-precision training; HBM capacity sets how big a model fits per chip; the matmul-native bias of all this silicon is part of why the transformer won — it maps perfectly onto these arrays. The co-evolution runs both ways: we design models for the hardware we have, and design hardware for the models that won.

Q1 What does a TPU's systolic array fundamentally optimize?

Matmul-native dataflow. A systolic array is a grid of multiply-accumulate cells through which operands rhythmically flow, so each loaded value is reused across many cells before leaving — maximizing arithmetic intensity by construction. That's why TPUs are so efficient on the matmul-heavy transformer. Trade-off: Google-Cloud-only and XLA, not CUDA.

Q2 Groq's LPU drops HBM entirely for on-chip SRAM and a deterministic pipeline. What does that buy and cost?

Speed for capacity. SRAM is far faster than HBM but far smaller, and a fully compiler-scheduled deterministic pipeline (no dynamic caches) makes latency predictable and very low — great for fast token streaming. The cost: a model won't fit on one chip, so it's sharded across many, and the design targets inference, not training.

Q3 What bottleneck is Cerebras's wafer-scale engine specifically deleting?

The network between chips. From the last lesson, crossing between chips is an order of magnitude slower than on-chip. Cerebras refuses to dice the wafer into separate chips — one wafer, ~4T transistors, ~900K cores — so a model that fits has no inter-chip network to traverse, with enormous on-wafer SRAM bandwidth. The constraint: power, cooling, and fitting the wafer's shape.

Q4 Why does the alternative-accelerator market concentrate on inference, not training?

Where the moat is thinnest. Training is custom-kernel-heavy and CUDA-deep — hard to leave. Inference is simpler and more standardized, so a chip optimized for latency or cost-per-token can win without reimplementing the whole CUDA stack. Hence "train on NVIDIA, serve on whatever's cheapest" — and why Groq, Cerebras, TPUs, and hyperscaler ASICs all attack inference first.

Q5 "There's no best chip." What's the actual decision rule?

Workload-roofline fit. Memory-bound decode at scale rewards bandwidth and cheap tokens; training rewards FLOPs, interconnect, and the CUDA stack; ultra-low-latency serving rewards deterministic SRAM chips. The right answer is best-for-this-workload-at-this-scale-under-this-power-and-software-constraint — exactly the lens behind your MI300X-vs-B200-vs-TPU evaluations.
Takeaway

GPUs are general; every alternative trades flexibility to kill one bottleneck — TPUs bake in the matmul, Groq's LPU goes deterministic SRAM for latency, Cerebras deletes the inter-chip network with a whole wafer. NVIDIA owns training via CUDA; inference is the contested frontier. There's no best chip, only best-for-this-workload — and hardware and models co-evolve.
