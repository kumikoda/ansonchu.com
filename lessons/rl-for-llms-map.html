How RL Actually Works for LLMs

You came in through DQN and Stable-Baselines — value-based, off-policy, small action spaces. The LLM world went a different direction: almost pure policy-gradient, and at the frontier it sheds the value function entirely. This is the top-down map; the last panel is the ladder back down to what you need to learn.

01 The premise

Why bolt RL onto a language model at all

Pretraining and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) are imitation — next-token prediction on text someone already wrote. That’s behavior cloning: the model can only copy demonstrated behavior, and it never sees the consequences of its own samples (the exposure-bias problem you’d recognize from imitation learning).

RL changes the objective from “predict the reference token” to “maximize a score on your own output.” That unlocks two things SFT can’t: optimizing for things you can judge but can’t easily write down as targets (helpfulness, correctness, preference), and learning from on-policy samples so the model improves the distribution it actually produces at test time.

Bridge from your DQN intuition DQN learned a value function and acted greedily. LLM RL almost never does that — the action space is too huge to argmax a Q over. It’s a policy-gradient world: you nudge the policy’s log-probs up or down by how good the sampled output was. That family is your main gap, and the ladder at the bottom targets it.

02 The classic recipe

RLHF in three stages

The InstructGPT pipeline that started the era. Three models, three steps:

the rl objective, in one linemaximize E[ R(x,y) ] − β·KL( π ‖ π_ref )
The KL leash is the whole trick Optimize a learned reward hard enough and the policy finds its blind spots — reward hacking (verbosity, sycophancy, weird tokens that spike R). The KL term is a leash to the reference distribution that keeps outputs language-shaped. Nearly every method below is partly a different answer to “how do we get reward without getting hacked?”

03 The simplification

DPO: delete the reward model and the RL loop

The dominant insight of 2023–24: the RLHF objective has a closed-form link between the optimal policy and the reward. Invert it and the reward model disappears — you can optimize directly on preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss. No sampling, no RM, no PPO.

RLHF (online)

  • sample from policy
  • score with reward model
  • PPO update + KL leash
  • 4 models, fiddly, can hack
  • learns from fresh samples ✓

DPO (offline)

  • fixed dataset of A≻B pairs
  • push up chosen, down rejected
  • one loss, no critic, no RM
  • stable + cheap to run
  • never sees new samples ✗
dpo loss — preferences become a log-prob gap−log σ( β·[ log π(y_win|x)/π_ref − log π(y_lose|x)/π_ref ] )

DPO and its cousins (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO) are the “direct alignment” family — they trade the online learning signal for enormous simplicity. Great for preference tuning; not what powers reasoning models, because being offline they can’t discover better behavior than what’s already in the pair data.

04 The frontier

RLVR + GRPO → reasoning models

The 2024–26 shift that produced o1, DeepSeek-R1 and the rest. Two moves stacked:

RLVR — replace the learned reward with a verifiable one

If a domain has a checkable answer — math (compare to ground truth), code (run the unit tests), formatted output (parse it) — you don’t need a hackable reward model. The reward is objective, cheap, and unhackable by construction. That lets you run RL at massive scale, and it’s where long chain-of-thought emerges: the model is rewarded only for the right final answer, and discovers that “thinking” longer first raises its hit rate.

GRPO — throw away the critic

DeepSeek’s trick, now everywhere. PPO needs a learned value network as a baseline. GRPO replaces it with the group itself: sample G answers to one prompt, score them all, and each answer’s advantage is just its reward normalized within the group.

The punchline that matters for you Strip GRPO down and it’s REINFORCE with a smart baseline — Monte-Carlo return (the verifier’s score), group-mean baseline, PPO-style clipping, KL to reference. No bootstrapping, no critic, no TD. The frontier is closer to the simplest policy-gradient method than to your DQN. That’s genuinely good news for your learning path.

The map in one line

LLM-RL treats the model as a policy, scores its own samples, and pushes good ones up — leashed to a reference. RLHF learns the score; the frontier verifies it. It’s all policy gradient, and the frontier is the simplest version of all.
