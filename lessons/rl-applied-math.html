RL for Math

Math is where reasoning RL was born — the R1 result, long chains of thought emerging from nothing but a correctness reward. But “correct” hides a fork: you can check a final answer, which is cheap and noisy, or verify a full proof, which is airtight and brutally hard. The whole field lives on that split.

01 The common path

Answer-checking and its blind spot

The workhorse of math RL: pose a problem with a known numeric/closed-form answer (GSM8K, MATH, AIME), let the model reason in natural language, extract the final answer, and reward an exact match. It’s cheap, parallel, and contamination-resistant when you use fresh problems (AIME 2025, released after training cutoffs, is a favorite clean test). This is the engine behind the headline results — frontier 32B reasoning models now score in the high-90s on AIME and earn IMO-level points.

The blind spot Answer-checking rewards the outcome, not the reasoning. A model can reach “42” by luck or by flawed logic that happens to land right — and get full reward. This is why a parallel track builds process verifiers (grade the steps) and LLM judges, and why benchmarks are moving to metrics that inspect the chain of thought, not just the final box.

02 The live debate

Does RL teach, or just sharpen?

The deepest open question in math RL, and it’s exactly your pass@1-vs-pass@k thread from the evals lesson. The provocative finding: as you let the base model sample k times, its best-of-k often catches up to the RL-trained model — suggesting RL mostly reranks abilities the base already had, rather than adding new ones.

Why you care This decides what RL is for. If it only sharpens, you’d invest in better base models and cheap best-of-k at inference. If it teaches valid reasoning (the CoT-pass@k evidence), RL earns its cost. The honest read in 2025–26: mostly elicits, but with a real teaching component — and how you measure determines which you see. Same lesson as LoRA’s “learns less,” viewed from the eval side.

03 The airtight path

Formal proof: the perfect, punishing verifier

The other branch abandons natural language for a formal proof assistant like Lean 4. The model writes a proof in a language where a checker mechanically confirms every step. The reward is flawless — no reward hacking is possible, because an invalid proof simply won’t type-check. This is the dream verifier… with a vicious catch.

Why it’s so hard — and the landmark The reward is brutally sparse: a proof is right only when fully complete, so the model gets nothing for being 90% of the way there — the hardest possible credit-assignment problem. The fix is search: tree search over proof steps guided by the model (MiniF2F is the standard olympiad benchmark in Lean). DeepMind’s AlphaProof combined an LLM with this kind of formal search and RL to reach silver-medal level at the 2024 IMO — the headline proof that formal-math RL can scale to real difficulty.

Q1 What's the blind spot of answer-checking as a math reward?

Outcome, not process. Matching the final answer is cheap and scalable, but a model can reach "42" by luck or broken reasoning that happens to land right — and collect full reward. That's why there's a parallel push toward process verifiers (grading steps) and CoT-aware metrics that inspect the reasoning, not just the boxed answer.

Q2 The pass@k debate asks whether RL "teaches or just sharpens." What does the evidence actually suggest?

It depends how you measure. Plain pass@k: the base's best-of-k catches the RL model → "just reranking." But require the reasoning to be valid (CoT-pass@k) and a gap persists out to k≈1024, especially on clean sets like AIME 2025. So RL teaches correct reasoning, not just lucky answers — the honest read is "mostly elicits, with a real teaching component."

Q3 Why can't a formal-proof (Lean) reward be reward-hacked?

Mechanical certainty. A formal proof assistant confirms every step; a proof that doesn't actually prove the theorem fails to type-check, so there's no gap between "passes the verifier" and "is correct." That makes it the perfect, un-gameable reward — the opposite of code's flaky-test problem. The cost is that writing checkable proofs is enormously hard.

Q4 What's the central difficulty of formal-proof RL, and how is it addressed?

Sparsity. A proof scores only when fully complete — 90% done earns nothing — making credit assignment maximally hard. The standard remedy is search: tree search over proof steps guided by the model (benchmarked on MiniF2F in Lean). AlphaProof combined an LLM with formal search and RL to reach silver-medal level at the 2024 IMO — proof the approach scales to real difficulty.
Takeaway

Math RL runs one engine over two verifiers: cheap, scalable answer-checking that's blind to reasoning, and airtight formal proof that's un-gameable but viciously sparse. The pass@k debate — does RL teach or just sharpen — is the field's live question, and the honest answer is "both, depending how you look." The frontier is making the reward see the reasoning, not just the result.
