PPO Core — Clipping & GAE

Actor-critic gave you the right gradient. PPO answers two practical questions that decide whether training actually works: how do I reuse a batch of samples without the policy lurching off a cliff, and how do I estimate the advantage without it being pure noise? Clipping handles the first; GAE handles the second.

01 The problem

Why vanilla policy gradient isn’t enough

REINFORCE is on-policy: collect samples, take one step, throw them away. Wasteful. You’d love to take many steps on one batch — but each step moves the policy away from the one that generated the batch, so the samples become stale and the gradient estimate goes wrong. Worse, RL has no safety net: one oversized update can collapse the policy into garbage it never recovers from, unlike supervised learning where the next batch just corrects you.

So the goal is a trust region: improve, but stay close enough to the old policy that the samples remain valid. TRPO did this with a hard KL constraint and heavy second-order math. PPO gets ~the same effect with a one-line trick and plain SGD — which is why it took over.

importance ratio — reweight old samples for the new policyrt(θ) = π_θ(at|st) / π_old(at|st)　　// =1 at the snapshot, drifts as θ moves
02 The signature idea

The clipped objective

Maximizing r_t·A_t alone lets the policy move arbitrarily far to exploit an advantage estimate that’s only locally trustworthy. PPO clips the ratio and takes the pessimistic (min) of clipped vs unclipped:

ppo-clipLCLIP = Et[ min( rt·At ,　clip(rt, 1−ε, 1+ε)·At ) ]　　ε ≈ 0.2
Why first-order beats TRPO in practice No constraint solver, no Fisher matrix, no conjugate gradients — just a min and a clip inside an ordinary SGD loop. PPO trades a little of TRPO’s theoretical rigor for something you can implement in ten lines and that just works. That trade is the entire reason it’s the default.

03 The owed lesson · TD ↔ Monte Carlo

GAE: the bias/variance dial for advantage

Back in Rung 3 the advantage was A = G − V (Monte Carlo): unbiased, because G is real reward — but high variance, because it sums many random future rewards. The opposite extreme uses one real reward and bootstraps the rest — the TD residual, which is exactly the TD error you used to train Q in DQN:

the td residual — your old friendδt = rt + γ·V(st+1) − V(st)　　// one real reward, then trust the critic

GAE interpolates between these two extremes with an exponentially-weighted sum of TD residuals, controlled by λ:

generalized advantage estimationAtGAE = Σl≥0 (γλ)l δt+l
λ=0 → δ_t (pure TD: biased, low variance)　·　λ=1 → G−V (Monte Carlo: unbiased, high variance)

05 Check yourself

One exercise

Suppose an action’s advantage is positive and, after a few epochs, its ratio r_t has climbed to 1.5 with ε = 0.2. What gradient does it contribute on the next step — and what does that tell you about why PPO can safely reuse a batch?

SOLVE Reveal the answer ▸

With A>0 and r=1.5 > 1+ε=1.2, the clipped term clip(r,0.8,1.2)·A = 1.2A is flat in r, and the min selects it (it’s the smaller). A flat objective has zero gradient — so this sample stops pushing the probability any higher. It’s already moved as far as PPO trusts on this batch.

That’s the mechanism: as samples hit their clip boundary they quietly switch off, so taking many epochs on one batch can’t run the policy away. The clip is the trust region — enforced sample-by-sample instead of as a global constraint. (Note the asymmetry from the main diagram: had r drifted the wrong way, the unclipped term would still produce a corrective gradient.)

Takeaway

PPO is actor-critic plus two pieces of engineering: GAE to make the advantage neither biased nor noisy, and a clipped ratio so you can reuse each batch without the policy bolting. Stabilization, not new theory.
