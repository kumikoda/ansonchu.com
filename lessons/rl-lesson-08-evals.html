Evals & Benchmarks

In RLVR the verifier is your reward and looks exactly like an eval — which is the trap. The instant a measure becomes the thing you optimize, it stops measuring. This lesson is about keeping an honest yardstick while you push numbers up, and reading what those numbers actually mean.

01 The central law

Reward is a target; an eval must not be

Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. RL is a Goodhart engine — its entire job is to maximize the reward number. So if your benchmark is the reward (or overlaps it), optimization will inflate the benchmark whether or not capability improved. The discipline is to keep a held-out yardstick the optimizer never touches.

the coupling that bites in rlvr verifier → used as reward // optimized against, hard
verifier → used as eval　 // must stay independent to mean anything
same checker for both = you’re grading your own homework
The fix is boring and non-negotiable Reserve tasks the trainer never sees: a held-out split, ideally freshly authored or private, scored by the same kind of checker but on different problems. Track it throughout training. If train reward rises while held-out is flat or falling, you’re hacking the reward, not learning.

02 The toolkit

Three families of eval, three trade-offs

LLM-as-judge is a reward model in disguise Using a strong model to grade is convenient and scalable — but it carries the same hackability as any learned reward: it has biases you can optimize into. Treat judge scores as a noisy proxy, calibrate against human spot-checks, and never let the judge be both grader and training reward unguarded.

03 Reading the metric

pass@1 vs pass@k: did RL teach, or just sharpen?

Two completions of the same metric tell very different stories. pass@1 = does it get it right on the first try? pass@k = does any of k samples succeed — the capability ceiling. RL famously lifts pass@1 hard. Whether it lifts pass@k is the live debate:

04 The bridge from Lesson 06

Contamination is measurement error

The data lesson’s villain returns as an eval failure. If test items leaked into training (or into the base model’s pretraining), the benchmark reports memorization on top of capability — the measured score sits above the true one, and you can’t tell by how much.

Design principles for an honest eval Held-out from training; contamination-controlled; hard enough to discriminate (a saturated benchmark near 100% measures nothing); representative of real use, not just convenient; and resistant to gaming. Increasingly the field leans on private, rotating, or continuously-refreshed sets precisely because public benchmarks leak and saturate.

05 Quiz · three

Check yourself

Q1 SOLVE In RLVR the verifier is both reward and the obvious eval. Spell out why that’s dangerous and the minimal fix. ▸

Danger: RL maximizes the reward by construction. If the same checker on the same items is your benchmark, any way the policy games the checker shows up as a benchmark gain — Goodhart in its purest form. You lose the ability to distinguish “got better at the task” from “got better at the checker.”

Minimal fix: reserve a held-out set of problems the trainer never sees, scored by the same kind of verifier but on disjoint items, and track it across training. The training reward can use the rest. The held-out curve diverging from train reward is your hacking alarm. Same checker, different data — that separation is the whole game.

Q2 SOLVE After RL, pass@1 goes 30%→55% but pass@256 moves only 72%→74%. What does this most likely mean — and why does it matter? ▸

It strongly suggests RL sharpened rather than taught. pass@256 ≈ the capability ceiling (can the model ever find the solution); it barely moved, so RL didn’t add solutions the base model couldn’t already occasionally produce. pass@1 leapt because RL concentrated probability mass onto those already-reachable correct solutions — making them reliable on the first try.

Why it matters: it reframes what RLVR is doing — eliciting and stabilizing latent capability, not creating new reasoning from nothing. Practical implications: the base model’s pass@k caps your ceiling (a stronger base or better SFT matters more than more RL once you’re near it), and if you need genuinely new capability, RL alone on the same base may not get you there. It’s an open, actively debated question — exact numbers and conclusions vary by setup, model scale, and task.

Q3 SOLVE Your LLM-judge scores climb steadily, but human spot-checks increasingly disagree. Which judge biases are the usual culprits? ▸

The judge is being optimized into, like any reward model. Usual suspects: length bias (longer, more elaborate answers rated higher regardless of quality); self-preference (the judge favors outputs in its own family’s style); position bias (in pairwise grading, favoring the first or second option); sycophancy / confident-tone bias (assertive, well-formatted answers scored up even when wrong); format gaming (markdown, headers, hedging phrases the judge associates with quality).

The divergence from humans is the tell that the policy found the judge’s blind spots. Mitigations: calibrate the judge against human labels, randomize option order, control for length, use rubric-based or reference-anchored grading, ensemble judges, and never let an unguarded judge be the sole training reward. A judge is a proxy with exploitable seams — treat it like one.

Takeaway

RL optimizes whatever you measure, so your real benchmark must be something the optimizer can’t touch — held-out, uncontaminated, hard to game. And read the metric you report: pass@1 rising is often sharpening, not new capability. The number is only as honest as the yardstick.
