Evals, The Deep Dive — and Harbor

Your first evals lesson made the argument that the eval is the product spec. This chapter is the practitioner's version: what an eval actually consists of, the statistics almost nobody applies (and how rankings dissolve when you do), the documented failure modes of LLM judges and arenas, the contamination war — and then Harbor, the framework that rebuilt agentic evaluation from scratch and, not coincidentally, doubles as an RL rollout engine. This is the direct substrate of your Systems capstone.

01 Anatomy

What an eval actually is

Strip any eval — MMLU to Terminal-Bench — and you find the same five organs. A task (input + success definition), an environment (nothing for a QA benchmark; a full container for an agent), a policy under test plus its harness (the scaffold: prompts, tools, retries — which is always part of what you're measuring, whether you admit it or not), a grader, and a metric that aggregates. Nearly every eval controversy is a defect in one organ being misread as a property of the model.

The grader is where the real design decision lives, and it's a ladder of trust vs. scalability:

The RL duality, stated plainly A grader run during training is called a reward; run after training, it's called an eval. Same artifact, two uses — which is why verifier quality was the recurring villain of your RL-for-code lesson, and why Harbor (§5) describes itself in one breath as a framework "for running agentic evals and generating RL rollouts." Build the grader once, use it both ways; corrupt it once, corrupt both.

02 The missing statistics

A benchmark score is a sample statistic. Treat it like one.

The single most common eval sin: reporting acc = 61.3% as if it were a physical constant. It's a sample mean over a finite question set, under one prompt template, one temperature, one seed — an estimate with variance from at least three sources: sampling (which N questions), generation (temperature > 0 makes the same model non-deterministic), and configuration (prompt phrasing and few-shot choice can swing scores by points — often more than the model gap being claimed).

The floor for honest reporting is the binomial standard error: for accuracy p on N independent questions, SE = √(p(1−p)/N). Concretely: 60% on a 500-question benchmark → SE ≈ 2.2%, so the 95% CI is roughly ±4.3 points. Two models at 61% and 63% on that benchmark are statistically indistinguishable. For anything beyond accuracy (clustered tasks, judge scores), bootstrap the CI; for claiming model A beats B, use a paired test on per-question outcomes — McNemar for pass/fail, Wilcoxon for scores — which is far more powerful than comparing two marginal means because it nets out per-question difficulty.

# pass@k, the right way (Codex-paper unbiased estimator).
# Naive "run k, count successes" overestimates. Instead: sample n ≥ k, count c correct:
def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    # P(at least one of k draws correct) = 1 - C(n-c, k)/C(n, k)
    if n - c < k: return 1.0
    return 1.0 - np.prod(1.0 - k / np.arange(n - c + 1, n + 1))
"Confidence intervals decide rankings, and nobody reads them" That's a direct diagnosis from current leaderboard methodology guides: on preference arenas, top models routinely sit inside overlapping 95% CIs, so treating rank #1 as materially better than rank #3 is trading on noise. The professional habit: never accept a leaderboard delta without asking N, the CI, and whether the comparison was paired. Your quant instincts transfer exactly — this is Sharpe-ratio-without-error-bars energy, and you already know how that ends.

03 LLM-as-judge

The judge is a model, with a model's pathologies

LLM-as-judge began with MT-Bench (2023), which reported >80% agreement between a GPT-4 judge and humans — and, in the same paper, documented a systematic position bias toward the answer presented first. That duality set the tone for everything since. The documented catalog:

Position bias (first/last answer favored — the fix, randomizing or averaging both orders, is mandatory, not optional). Verbosity/style bias (longer, confidently formatted answers win even at equal substance — the same bias that lets labs tune for "arena appeal"). Self-preference (judges favor outputs from their own family; Arena-Hard's GPT-4 judge inflating GPT-4-family scores is the canonical case — never let a model family judge itself on a leaderboard that matters).

Recent large-scale audits sharpened this into two findings worth internalizing. First, kappa deflation: across 21 judges, raw exact-match agreement exceeds chance-corrected agreement (Cohen's κ) by 33.8–41.2 percentage points — much of that comforting "80% agreement" is agreement you'd get by chance on skewed labels. Second, the consistency–bias paradox: some judges show test-retest reliability ≥ 0.95 while carrying severe position bias — the most reproducible judges are among the least valid. A judge can be perfectly consistent and consistently wrong. Reliability is not validity; audit both.

Mitigations that actually work Randomize positions and average. Use rubrics with explicit per-criterion prompts rather than holistic "which is better" (auditable at the criterion level — exactly how COMPOSITE-style benchmarks structure criteria.json). Prefer ground-truth grading where it exists — LiveBench's core argument, and why its ground-truth scores expose judge-inflated models. And for rigor: treat the judge as a noisy classifier, estimate its sensitivity/specificity on a small human-labeled calibration set, and apply the bias-corrected estimator — recent methods restore ~95% CI coverage on Arena win-rates this way. A judge is an instrument; calibrate it like one.

04 Arenas & the contamination war

Preference leaderboards, and keeping the test set out of the training set

Chatbot Arena (LMArena) aggregates 6M+ blind pairwise human votes. Mechanically it matters that in late 2023 it moved from online Elo to a Bradley–Terry maximum-likelihood fit over the full match history — BT is the right model when model strength is static, yields meaningful CIs (bootstrapped with 1,000 permutations), and its ratings are population-relative with no fixed ceiling (the 2026 frontier sits ~1450–1560). Read arenas for what they uniquely measure — aggregate human preference and conversational style — and distrust them for capability: style bias is baked into the signal, and small Elo gaps inside overlapping CIs are noise.

Contamination is the other structural threat: static public benchmarks leak into pretraining corpora, and scores inflate without capability. The defenses form a spectrum: private held-out sets (trust the maintainer), canary strings (detect naive inclusion), functional/perturbed variants (regenerate numbers, see if the score survives), and living benchmarks — LiveBench's design: refresh questions monthly from fresh sources (new arXiv papers, news, recent competitions) and grade against ground truth, making both contamination and judge bias structurally hard. Its ~0.9 correlation with arenas, with the divergences explained by known judge/style biases, is a tidy validation of the whole critique above. The treadmill is real regardless: MMLU saturating → HLE; SWE-bench Verified climbing fast; benchmarks are consumables now.

05 Harbor

Harbor: agentic evaluation, rebuilt as infrastructure

Everything above gets an order of magnitude harder when the eval is agentic — because the eval stops being a dataset and becomes a distributed system: a container per attempt, an agent installed and run inside it, timeouts, flaky base images, external services drifting, verifiers executing in a dirty environment. Terminal-Bench 1.0 (May 2025) lived this: rapid adoption, then community-identified tasks that were poorly specified or unstable because external services changed. The lesson generalizes: in agentic evals, most of your error bar can be the harness, not the model. A recent study quantified it perfectly — third-party agents showed 4.5–21.5% infrastructure error rates (mostly toolchain installs failing on end-of-life base images) versus 3.5–5.5% for the harness-native agent. Those points come off the score before the model even acts.

Harbor is the Terminal-Bench team's answer: the original harness rewritten from the ground up for reliability, observability, scalability, and performance — used internally to run tens of thousands of rollouts while building Terminal-Bench 2.0, then released publicly. The design in brief:

The task-and-trial abstraction. Each task is a self-contained package: an instruction.md (the prompt layer), an environment definition (Docker), and executable verification assets — from deterministic test scripts to structured rubric criteria in tests/criteria.json, where each criterion can name its grader type (including llm-judge with an explicit semantic prompt), keeping grader behavior auditable per criterion. A trial is one attempt: fresh container, agent injected, traces and verifier results collected as artifacts. Authoring is cleanly separated from orchestration.

Agent-agnostic execution. Harbor runs its native agent (Terminus-2, a deliberately minimal ReAct loop issuing tmux keystrokes to a persistent shell) but also injects and evaluates Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, OpenHands — anything subclassing its agent base classes. Scale-out is built in: local Docker concurrency or cloud providers (Daytona, Modal) for large parallel sweeps.

# the whole loop, from the docs — sanity-check the benchmark with oracle solutions:
uv run harbor run --dataset terminal-bench@2.0 --agent oracle --n-concurrent 4

# then evaluate a real agent/model:
export ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=<KEY>
uv run harbor run --dataset terminal-bench@2.0 \
    --agent claude-code --model anthropic/claude-opus-4-1 --n-concurrent 4

Note the --agent oracle idiom: run the known-good solutions through the full pipeline first. If oracle doesn't score 100%, your harness — not any model — is broken. That's eval hygiene as a first-class feature.

Terminal-Bench 2.0 itself shows what the quality bar costs: 89 tasks across 16 categories (SWE, security, scientific computing, data science, debugging…), each subjected to several hours of manual plus LLM-assisted validation to be solvable, realistic, and stable. At launch the leaderboard's best — a GPT-5 Codex CLI variant — sat at 49.6%, with GPT-5 variants and Claude-Sonnet-4.5-based agents close behind: deliberately unsaturated headroom.

The eval–RL duality, made literal — and its boundary Harbor's self-description is the duality from §1 shipped as software: evals and RL rollouts from the same substrate. The container + verifier that grades an agent is precisely what generates rewarded trajectories for training — your harness lesson and this one are the same machine. One boundary worth knowing (flagged by the Polar work): Harbor launches native harnesses against external model endpoints but doesn't proxy the model traffic — so it hands you trajectories and scores, not token IDs, logprobs, and loss masks. Evaluating a custom Qwen checkpoint through Claude Code needs an Anthropic-compatible endpoint; wiring rollouts directly into an RL trainer takes an extra layer at that model boundary. Evaluation-first, training-adjacent.

06 Building one

The checklist for your Systems capstone

Everything in this chapter compresses into the discipline for building an eval worth trusting — which is exactly your capstone (an environment + eval, on Harbor or Prime Intellect's hub):

1. Grade against ground truth wherever possible; where a judge is unavoidable, use per-criterion rubrics, randomize positions, and never let a family judge itself. 2. Validate every task — run oracle solutions through the full pipeline; a task no reference solution passes is measuring your bugs. 3. Design for contamination — hold out, perturb, or plan refresh from day one; assume anything public is in the next pretraining run. 4. Report SE/CIs and use paired tests; state N; resist ranking inside overlapping intervals. 5. Separate harness error from model error — track infrastructure failures as their own category, or they silently become "model" failures. 6. Leave headroom — a benchmark launched near 50% for the best agent (TB2's choice) stays informative for years; one launched at 90% is already dead.

Q1 Two models score 61% and 63% on a 500-question benchmark. What's the correct read?

A score is a sample mean. SE = √(p(1−p)/N) ≈ √(0.6·0.4/500) ≈ 2.2%. Beyond sampling error there's generation variance (temperature) and configuration variance (prompt/few-shot choices, often worth more than 2 points alone). Paired tests on per-question outcomes net out item difficulty and are the honest way to compare — the same logic as your paired backtest comparisons in quant work.

Q2 What is "kappa deflation" in LLM-as-judge audits?

Agreement isn't validity. On skewed label distributions, a judge can match humans often just by predicting the majority. κ corrects for that, and the gap between exact-match and κ (34–41 points!) shows how inflated raw agreement is. Paired finding: the consistency-bias paradox — judges with ≥0.95 test-retest reliability carrying severe position bias. Perfectly consistent, consistently wrong.

Q3 Why did Chatbot Arena move from online Elo to Bradley–Terry, and what should you still distrust?

Better statistics, same epistemics. Online Elo is order-dependent and noisy; Bradley–Terry MLE over all pairwise votes is the right estimator for static strengths and yields real CIs (LMArena bootstraps with 1,000 permutations). But the signal itself still prefers longer, confident answers (why labs tune for "arena appeal"), and frontier models routinely sit within overlapping CIs — rank #1 vs #3 is often noise.

Q4 What's LiveBench's two-part design against the two big corruption vectors?

Living benchmark + objective grading. New questions monthly from recent papers/news/competitions can't be in anyone's pretraining set; ground-truth answers remove the judge. Validation: ~0.9 correlation with arenas overall, with the deviations explained — GPT-4-judged Arena-Hard inflating GPT-4-family models, style-favored models doing better on human votes than on ground truth.

Q5 Why does Harbor ship an --agent oracle mode, and what problem does it target?

Separate harness error from model error. In agentic evals the container, toolchain installs, timeouts, and stale base images fail before the model acts — measured at 4.5–21.5% for third-party agents vs 3.5–5.5% for the native one. Running oracle solutions first proves the pipeline; TB2's per-task hours of validation and Harbor's trial artifacts exist for the same reason. In agentic evals, most of your error bar can be the harness.

Q6 What is the eval–RL duality, and where is Harbor's boundary in it?

One artifact, two uses, one seam. Harbor's container+verifier substrate produces scored trajectories that serve as evals or RL rollouts — the duality your harness lesson kept circling, shipped as software. The boundary (highlighted by Polar): Harbor launches native harnesses against external endpoints rather than mediating the model traffic, so plugging its rollouts straight into a trainer requires an extra proxy layer at the model boundary. Evaluation-first, training-adjacent.
Takeaway

An eval is five organs — task, environment, harness, grader, metric — and every scandal is one organ failing. Scores are sample statistics (SEs, paired tests, or it didn't happen); judges are noisy instruments with documented biases (calibrate, randomize, never self-judge); arenas measure preference, not capability; contamination makes benchmarks consumables. And agentic evals are distributed systems, which is why Harbor exists: validated task packages, oracle runs to prove the pipeline, per-criterion auditable graders, cloud-scale trials — the same substrate that grades agents also generates RL rollouts. Build your capstone eval like this and it's a real contribution.
