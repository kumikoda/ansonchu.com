Data & Data Engineering

Here’s the reframe that trips up everyone arriving from supervised learning: in online RL, your dataset is not input–output pairs. It’s a distribution of prompts plus a way to score answers. The model manufactures its own targets by sampling. Almost every data-engineering decision flows from that one shift.

01 The core shift

Online RL data is a loop, not a file

In supervised learning the dataset sits still and you iterate over it. In online RL the policy generates the data each step, so the training distribution moves as the model improves. Your “dataset” is really two things: a pool of prompts, and a reward function. The (prompt, completion, score) triples that actually drive gradients are minted fresh every iteration and then discarded.

The practical upshot for your build For an RLVR experiment you don’t collect answers — you collect problems with checkable solutions and write a verifier. GSM8K/MATH ship with gold final answers; code tasks ship with unit tests. Your data job is curating prompts and hardening the checker, not labeling completions.

02 The engineering

The pipeline that earns its keep

Raw problem sets are dirty. The pipeline that turns them into a training-grade prompt set is unglamorous and decisive — most “my RL won’t improve” stories are really data stories.

Decontamination is the one people skip If a test problem leaks into training, your benchmark measures memorization, not capability — and simple n-gram matching misses paraphrases, translations, and reorderings. Worse, you can’t scrub what’s already in the base model’s pretraining. This is the load-bearing link to the evals lesson: contamination is measurement error you bake in at the data stage.

03 Connecting to GRPO

Difficulty is a data-engineering problem

Remember the closing exercise from the GRPO lesson: a group that’s all-correct or all-wrong has zero advantage and teaches nothing. That makes prompt difficulty a first-class data concern. The useful prompts are the ones where the current policy sometimes succeeds — that’s where the group disagrees and the gradient is largest.

04 Quiz · three this time

Check yourself

Q1 SOLVE Why can online RLVR need far fewer human labels than SFT — yet far more compute? ▸

Fewer labels: SFT needs a written gold answer for every prompt (expensive human demonstration). RLVR needs only the prompt plus a checker — the model writes its own completions, and a verifier scores them. For math you need the final answer (often already in the dataset), not a worked solution; for code, just the tests. Human effort collapses to “collect problems + harden the verifier.”

More compute: you sample G completions per prompt every iteration, repeatedly, against a moving policy. You’ve traded human annotation cost for inference/rollout cost — which is exactly why the frontier moved toward verifiable domains where that trade is cheap and the signal is clean.

Q2 SOLVE You decontaminate training against the benchmark with n-gram matching, but eval scores still look too good. Name three leakage paths n-grams miss. ▸

Any three of: (1) paraphrase / translation — same problem, different surface words, so no shared n-grams; (2) semantic duplicates — structurally identical problems with different numbers or names; (3) base-model pretraining contamination — the test items were in the pretraining corpus, which you cannot scrub post-hoc; (4) solution leakage — the answer appears somewhere in training even if the question doesn’t; (5) reformatting — reordered multiple-choice options or restructured prompts.

The lesson: n-gram decontamination is necessary but weak. Embedding-based near-duplicate detection catches more, and honest reporting means treating any benchmark the base model may have seen with suspicion. This is precisely why held-out and freshly-authored evals matter — the bridge to Lesson 08.

Q3 SOLVE Training plateaus on 100k math prompts. Difficulty-filtering to the “hard” ones is tempting — what’s the risk, and what’s the better move? ▸

The risk: filter to only hard problems and many groups become all-wrong — unanimous failure, Â=0, no gradient. You’d recreate the plateau from the other tail. You also strip the easier prompts that supply positive examples and preserve already-learned capability, risking regression.

The better move: target the learnable band — prompts the current policy solves part of the time (say, pass-rate roughly 0.2–0.8 across the group) — and re-estimate difficulty as the policy improves, because the band drifts toward harder problems over training. In practice: dynamic sampling / online filtering that drops both unanimous-pass and unanimous-fail groups, keeping a difficulty distribution rather than a hardness cliff. This is curriculum learning, done with the model’s own pass-rates as the difficulty signal.

Takeaway

In online RL the dataset is a prompt distribution and a scorer, not a pile of answers. Your real data work is curating prompts at the right difficulty, hardening the verifier, and decontaminating ruthlessly — because the model supplies everything else itself.
