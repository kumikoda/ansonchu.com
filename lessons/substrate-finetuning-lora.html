Fine-Tuning & LoRA

Every stage after pretraining — SFT, RLHF, RLVR — is fine-tuning. The question is just how many weights you’re allowed to move. LoRA’s answer: almost none. It trains a tiny low-rank patch and freezes the rest, which is exactly why Tinker can run your jobs cheaply and share one GPU pool across many of them.

02 Why not just full fine-tune

The memory wall

Full fine-tuning’s cost isn’t the weights — it’s everything training drags along per weight. For each trainable parameter you also store a gradient and the optimizer’s state (Adam keeps two: momentum and variance). That’s why the memory balloons to several times the model size:

03 The idea

Fine-tuning updates are low-rank

The insight behind LoRA: when you adapt a pretrained model, the change to a weight matrix, ΔW, has low intrinsic rank — you don’t need a full-rank update to specialize it. So don’t learn the full ΔW; factor it into two skinny matrices:

the low-rank update h = Wx + ΔWx = Wx + B·A·x　　B: d×r, A: r×k, r ≪ d,k
// often scaled by α/r; trainable params drop from d·k to r·(d+k)

For a 4096×4096 layer, full ΔW is ~16M parameters; LoRA at r=8 is ~65K — roughly 250× fewer. And a deliberate init trick: A is random, B starts at zero, so BA = 0 at step one. Training begins exactly at the pretrained model and learns a correction outward from there — no disruptive jump.

Why low-rank is enough A pretrained model already “knows” the relevant features; adapting to a task mostly means re-weighting a few directions, not relearning the matrix. Empirically the update lives in a low-dimensional subspace, so a rank-8 or rank-16 patch captures most of what full fine-tuning would do — for style, format, and instruction-following especially.

05 Knobs & tradeoffs

Rank, scale, and “learns less, forgets less”

The hyperparameters you’ll actually touch: rank r sets adapter capacity (8–64 typical; higher for harder shifts or more data); α scales the update (effective α/r); and which layers get adapters (attention projections at minimum, often all linear layers for better quality). QLoRA goes further — quantize the frozen base to 4-bit and put LoRA on top — which is how people fine-tune 65B+ models on a single GPU.

The tradeoff to hold in your head, well-supported empirically: LoRA learns less and forgets less. Its smaller update space makes it a weaker learner for absorbing large amounts of genuinely new knowledge or big distribution shifts — but that same constraint means it disturbs the base model less, so it preserves existing capabilities better (less catastrophic forgetting).

How this lands for your RL runs Most RLVR experiments — the GRPO runs you’re heading toward — are fine on LoRA: you’re eliciting and sharpening behavior the base already has (recall pass@1-vs-pass@k from the evals lesson), not injecting new knowledge. That’s exactly LoRA’s strong zone, which is why Tinker being LoRA-only is rarely the bottleneck for this kind of work. Reach for full fine-tuning only when you genuinely need to move the capability ceiling.

Q1 Beyond the weights themselves, what makes full fine-tuning so memory-hungry?

Optimizer states + gradients per weight. Adam keeps momentum and variance for every trainable parameter, plus a gradient — so full fine-tuning needs roughly 3–4× the model size in trainable memory. LoRA freezes the weights, so they need no gradient and no optimizer state; you only pay that cost on the tiny adapter. That's the whole memory win.

Q2 LoRA initializes B to zero (and A random). What does that guarantee?

A clean start. With B = 0, the product BA = 0 at initialization, so the LoRA path contributes nothing on the first step — the model behaves identically to the frozen base, then learns a correction outward from there. No disruptive jump, no risk of wrecking pretrained behavior before learning anything.

Q3 How can LoRA add zero inference latency?

Merge it. After training, fold the low-rank update into the original: W′ = W + BA. Now the forward pass is one matmul, identical in cost to the base model — the adapter has vanished into W. (Or you keep them separate on purpose, trading that for the ability to hot-swap many adapters on one base.)

Q4 "LoRA learns less and forgets less." For which job is that tradeoff most in LoRA's favor?

Eliciting, not injecting. When you're sharpening behavior the base already has — instruction-following, formatting, or RLVR that concentrates probability on solutions the model can already sometimes find (the pass@1-vs-pass@k story) — LoRA's smaller update space is plenty, and its gentleness preserves base capabilities. It's weaker exactly when you need to pour in lots of genuinely new knowledge; that's where full fine-tuning earns its cost.
Takeaway

Fine-tuning is post-training; the only question is how many weights move. LoRA freezes the model and trains a tiny low-rank patch — cheap memory, mergeable for free inference, swappable across tasks. It learns a little less and forgets a lot less, which makes it the right default for everything short of injecting deep new knowledge.
