Hardware · the network INTERCONNECT & COLLECTIVES

Making 100,000 GPUs Act Like One

A frontier model doesn’t fit on one chip — not close. So training is a distributed-systems problem, and the binding constraint becomes the wires between the chips. This is the layer where “the network is the computer” stops being a slogan: which parallelism you can use is decided entirely by how fast your GPUs can talk.

01 The two networks

Scale-up vs scale-out

There are two completely different networks in a modern cluster, and conflating them is the classic mistake. Scale-up is NVLink + NVSwitch: a small number of GPUs wired into one coherent, ultra-fast domain that behaves like a single giant accelerator. Scale-out is InfiniBand or Ethernet (RoCE): a slower, switched fabric reaching across thousands of nodes.

Why the rack keeps growing The history is a march outward: NVLink went from 8 GPUs per node → 72 per rack (NVL72) → up to 576 in one fabric. Each expansion of the coherent domain means more of the model can live on the fast wires. That’s the entire strategic point of rack-scale systems, and why the open camp is racing to match it — UALink (ratified 2025; AMD/Broadcom/Google/Intel/Meta/Microsoft) plus Ultra Ethernet, with AMD’s Helios rack putting 72 MI400-class GPUs in a UALink domain.

02 What flows on the wires

Collectives, and why parallelism is a placement problem

GPUs don’t send random messages — they run collective operations. The big one is all-reduce: every GPU sums its gradients with every other’s, every step. Add all-gather / reduce-scatter (for sharded weights) and all-to-all (for MoE routing). These collectives, run by NCCL, are most of what the network does — and their cost is what you’re fighting.

Each way of splitting a model has a signature communication pattern, and that signature dictates which network it’s allowed to run on:

The one rule that organizes it all Put the chattiest parallelism on the fastest wires. Tensor and expert parallelism all-reduce or all-to-all every single layer, so they must stay inside the NVLink domain; pipeline and data parallelism communicate rarely, so they can stretch across the slow InfiniBand fabric between racks. A real training run is this 3D/4D stack — TP×EP inside the rack, PP×DP across racks — laid out to respect the bandwidth cliff. NVL72’s all-to-all topology exists specifically to make MoE expert-parallel traffic cheap.

03 The bottleneck

Communication is the tax — hide it or pay it

Every byte spent on a collective is a byte not spent computing. As clusters grow, this communication tax grows with them, and at some point the network — not the GPUs — caps your throughput. The two defenses: overlap communication with computation (start the gradient all-reduce while later layers still compute), and grow the coherent domain so more traffic stays on NVLink. The whole NVL72-and-beyond arms race is the second strategy made physical.

This is your harness lesson, at the wire level The weight-sync and actor-learner shuffles from the harness lesson are these collectives. When you disaggregate generation from training, you’re deciding which pools share an NVLink domain and which talk over the slow fabric — a topology decision. And topology-aware schedulers (Slurm’s block-topology plugin) exist precisely because the NVLink-vs-InfiniBand gap is too large to ignore when placing jobs. For your rack-scale build, this is the design surface: the wiring is the architecture.

Q1 What's the difference between scale-up and scale-out networking?

Two different networks. Scale-up wires a few dozen GPUs into one coherent, ~18× faster NVLink domain that acts like a single giant accelerator (NVL72 = 72 GPUs, 130 TB/s). Scale-out is the InfiniBand/Ethernet fat-tree that connects racks across the datacenter — far more reach, far less bandwidth. Conflating them is the classic distributed-training mistake.

Q2 Why must tensor parallelism (TP) stay inside the NVLink domain while data parallelism (DP) can span racks?

Communication signature. TP splits each matmul, so it all-reduces partial results twice every layer — enormous, constant traffic that only NVLink-grade bandwidth can hide. DP replicates the model and all-reduces gradients just once per step — infrequent enough to survive InfiniBand latency. The rule: chattiest parallelism on the fastest wires.

Q3 Which collective operation does MoE expert parallelism depend on, and why does NVL72's topology matter for it?

All-to-all. In MoE, each token is routed to its chosen experts, which live on different GPUs — so every device exchanges with every other (all-to-all), every MoE layer. NVL72's all-to-all NVLink fabric (full bisection bandwidth between any GPU pair) is built precisely to make this expert-parallel traffic affordable, which is why frontier MoE training leans on rack-scale domains.

Q4 As clusters scale, what becomes the throughput bottleneck, and what are the two defenses?

The network is the tax. Every byte on a collective is a byte not computing, and that tax grows with cluster size until the network caps throughput. You fight it by overlapping communication with computation (e.g. all-reduce earlier layers' grads while later layers still compute) and by enlarging the NVLink domain so more traffic stays on the fast wires — exactly what the NVL72-and-beyond arms race is doing.
Takeaway

Training a frontier model is a networking problem: every hop off the chip is a ~10–20× bandwidth cliff, so you split the model to keep its chattiest pieces (tensor, expert parallel) on the fast NVLink domain and stretch the quiet pieces (pipeline, data parallel) across the InfiniBand fabric. Collectives are the traffic, the network is the tax, and growing the coherent rack is how the industry keeps paying it.
