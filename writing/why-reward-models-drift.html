Why reward models drift, and what to do about it

2026-08-09 · 6 min · alignment

This is an example post — replace the body with your own. It exists so you can see how headings, lists, code, quotes, and footnotes look on the site.

A reward model is a photograph of human preference taken at one instant. The policy you train against it is not a photograph; it is a search process actively looking for the cheapest inputs that score well. The moment you start optimizing, the two objects begin to come apart, and most of the failures people call "reward hacking" are really just this gap widening faster than anyone is measuring it.

The mechanism

Concretely, three things happen at once:

  • The policy shifts its output distribution toward regions the reward model rarely saw during training.
  • In those regions the reward model's estimates are, by construction, least reliable.
  • The optimizer preferentially exploits exactly the places where the estimate is high and wrong.

You can write the pressure down. If r̂ is the learned reward and r is the thing we actually care about, KL-regularized optimization is climbing r̂ − β·KL(π‖π₀), and nothing in that objective knows about the error r̂ − r off-distribution.

The reward model isn't wrong on the training set. It's wrong on the set the policy is about to invent.

A cheap diagnostic

Before reaching for anything fancy, log the gap. Hold out a small stream of fresh human labels and, each eval step, compare them against the reward model's score on the current policy's samples:

drift = mean(reward_model(sample) - human_label(sample))
# rising drift with rising reward = you are training the gap, not the goal

If drift climbs while your headline reward climbs, the reward number is no longer evidence of anything. This one plot catches most of the embarrassing failures before they ship.

What actually helps

  1. Keep the labeler in the loop. Periodically refresh preference data on the current policy's outputs, not a frozen dataset. The photograph has to be re-taken.
  2. Regularize toward the reference, and mean it. A larger β buys reliability at the cost of ceiling. Most teams set it once and never revisit.
  3. Ensemble and treat disagreement as uncertainty. Where reward models disagree, the optimizer should be discouraged, not rewarded.

None of this makes the gap go away. It just keeps the gap observable, which is the whole game.1

  1. The gap is fundamental: you cannot optimize a proxy arbitrarily hard and expect it to keep tracking the target. Naming it "drift" is just a way to make it show up on a dashboard. ↩
